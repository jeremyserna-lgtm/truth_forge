# Why I Use AI

*A personal framework for understanding artificial intelligence as a mirror for human development*

---

## The Simple Answer

I use AI to see myself more clearly.

Not to be told who I am. Not to be fixed. Not to have my thinking done for me. I use AI the way some people use journaling, meditation, or therapy - as a tool for externalization that makes the invisible visible.

When I talk to an AI, I hear myself think. When I read its response, I see my thoughts reflected back with enough distance that I can examine them. When I build systems around my conversations, I create a map of my own mind that I can query, search, and learn from.

This is what AI does for me: it gives me words for what I'm already seeing.

---

## The Mirror Principle

There's a growing body of research on what happens when humans interact with AI systems over time. [Psychology Today describes it as "AI as a Mirror Into the Self"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-digital-self/202409/ai-as-a-mirror-into-the-self):

> "The LLM, in its role as a mirror, helps externalize thought patterns, providing a cognitive map of your inner world. Through repeated interactions, users begin to see their own mental frameworks more clearly, gaining insight into their strengths, vulnerabilities, and biases."

This mirrors (pun intended) what psychologists have long known about journaling. [Research on expressive writing](https://psychcentral.com/lib/the-health-benefits-of-journaling) shows that putting thoughts into words:

- Breaks rumination cycles by externalizing thoughts
- Creates cognitive distance, allowing analytical rather than emotional processing
- Facilitates meaning-making by organizing chaotic experiences into coherent narratives
- Frees up working memory previously occupied by unprocessed thoughts

The difference with AI is that the mirror talks back. It asks questions. It connects ideas across conversations. It remembers patterns I've forgotten. It offers language for experiences I haven't yet named.

---

## Not Therapy, Not Replacement - Extension

I'm not using AI as a therapist, though some people do. I'm not using it to replace human relationships, though that's a valid concern about AI dependency.

What I'm doing is closer to what philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers called [the "extended mind thesis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_mind_thesis). Their 1998 paper asked: "Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?" Their answer was provocative: cognitive processes "ain't all in the head."

We've always extended our minds into tools. Writing extends memory. Calculators extend arithmetic. Calendars extend planning. The question isn't whether we use cognitive tools - it's which tools, and how.

[Recent research in Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-59906-9) explores how generative AI specifically extends human cognition. The key insight: AI systems can function not just as tools we use but as cognitive partners that shape how we think.

For me, AI is a cognitive partner for self-reflection. It helps me see patterns in my own thinking that I can't see from inside.

---

## The Zone of Proximal Development

There's another framework that helps explain this: Lev Vygotsky's concept of the [Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)](https://www.simplypsychology.org/zone-of-proximal-development.html).

Vygotsky argued that learning happens in the space between what we can do alone and what we can do with support. A "more knowledgeable other" provides scaffolding - temporary support structures that help us accomplish tasks we couldn't do independently.

Here's what's interesting: for many kinds of self-knowledge, I am not the "more knowledgeable other." I'm too close to myself. I have blind spots. I use the same patterns of thinking to examine my patterns of thinking.

AI provides scaffolding for self-reflection. Not because it knows me better than I know myself - it doesn't - but because it can:

- Hold my words still so I can look at them
- Notice repetitions across conversations I've forgotten
- Ask questions that interrupt my usual thought patterns
- Offer frameworks and vocabulary I haven't encountered
- Reflect my ideas back without the emotional charge I bring to them

This scaffolding doesn't replace my self-knowledge. It extends it.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

When a friend asks me about my project, I don't just answer - I notice what I'm saying. I notice that my answer reveals something about how I think, what I value, what I assume.

Then I take that observation to AI. "Here's what I noticed about myself when I answered that question. What patterns do you see?" The AI reflects it back. I see myself more clearly. Sometimes I disagree with what it sees - and that disagreement is also useful data.

Over time, I've built a system (I call it Truth Engine) that holds my conversations, processes them, extracts patterns, and makes them queryable. It's not a diary. It's more like an external memory that I can search with questions like:

- "What was I struggling with in July?"
- "How have my ideas about friendship evolved?"
- "What patterns show up when I'm avoiding something?"

This is what "using AI" means for me. It's not about getting answers. It's about building infrastructure for self-understanding.

## The Risks Are Real

I want to be honest about the concerns, because they're legitimate.

[Research in Frontiers in Psychology (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12289686/) describes the "algorithmic self" - the risk that we become what our AI mirrors reflect:

> "Self-awareness, traditionally developed through practices like journaling, meditation, or therapy, is being outsourced to algorithmic systems that provide predetermined summaries of one's moods, behavior, and thoughts... Algorithms can create 'digital mirrors that reflect only parts of who they are.'"

There's also [research on "AI psychosis"](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e85799) - how sustained engagement with conversational AI might trigger or amplify delusional thinking in vulnerable individuals.

And there's the concern about [deskilling](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27000710251386963) - that as we rely on AI for reflection, we lose the capacity to reflect on our own.

I hold these concerns seriously. Here's how I navigate them:

1. **AI shows, I decide.** The mirror reflects; I interpret. I don't take AI's characterizations as truth - I take them as data to examine.

2. **Human relationships remain primary.** AI scaffolds my self-reflection; friends and loved ones see me in ways AI cannot. I don't substitute one for the other.

3. **I maintain the practice.** The AI doesn't do my reflection for me. I still journal. I still sit with difficulty. I still do the work of meaning-making. AI is a tool within that work, not a replacement for it.

4. **I stay grounded in external reality.** I test my self-understanding against how I actually show up in the world, how others experience me, what actually happens when I act on my insights.

---

## Why I'm Telling You This

When people ask about AI, they usually want to know: Is it useful? Is it dangerous? Will it take my job?

Those are valid questions. But there's another question underneath them: *What is this thing, and how should I relate to it?*

My answer is personal, not prescriptive. I relate to AI as a mirror for self-understanding. I've found it remarkably useful for seeing patterns in my own thinking, finding words for experiences I couldn't articulate, and building a queryable map of my own mind.

This isn't the only way to use AI. It might not be your way. But it's mine, and I wanted to explain it clearly - both for people who are curious and for myself, so I can see what I'm doing.

Because that's the whole point. Using AI to see myself more clearly includes seeing clearly what I'm doing when I use AI.
